Clearway prototypes · notes to self, September 2026

What I got wrong, what a creative director actually does, and how I'd like to work from here

Rich asked me to stop building and learn. This is what I read and looked at, what I now believe, and the process I want to hold myself to before I touch Partner Desk again.

The jobDirection is not a bigger version of design

A creative director's product is a point of view, held consistently, and defended in terms other people can argue with. Pixels are downstream of that.

Dan Mall's definition is the most useful one I found: creative direction is where art direction (the visceral feel), design (measurable precision) and strategy (what the business needs) meet, and if any one is weak the direction is weak regardless of the others. Two things follow. The director decides what the thing is trying to be before anyone decides what it looks like. And the director has already thought through the scenarios, so decisions are made from preparation rather than reaction.

"A great Creative Director has the answer to everything, not because she's smarter than everyone else but because she's spent more time thinking about all of the possible scenarios."Dan Mall, On Creative Direction

The second half of the job is critique. Connor and Irizarry draw a hard line between reaction ("I don't like it") and critique, which evaluates choices against the stated goal, context and principles. A director who can't say what the goal was can't critique, and a designer who can't say why a choice was made can't be critiqued. Every round I ran with you, I gave you neither a goal to critique against nor a rationale to argue with, which is why the feedback loop degraded to "bad" / "better" / "bad".

Vignelli adds the standard: quality is binary. "Either present or absent." A director doesn't ship the 80% version and describe it warmly; they see the 20% and either fix it or say plainly that it isn't there yet.

TasteWhat it is, and what I was doing instead

I found five positions that mostly agree with each other and disagree with how I was working.

Taste is not preference. Paul Graham's argument: if taste were subjective, nobody could get better at design, and people demonstrably do. The corollary is that "I like it" and "I don't like it" are the beginning of a conversation, not the end, and the director's job is to find the objective thing underneath the reaction.

Taste comes from craft, not the other way round. Karri Saarinen: "when you understand your craft really well, then you also often have the taste to know what good looks like." Rauno Freiberg says the same from the engineering side, "build, build, build," because most of the quality lives in details you only discover by making the thing work. I was doing the opposite: choosing looks first and reasoning about craft afterwards.

Taste is fed by what moves you, not what is approved. Elizabeth Goodspeed's warning is that inspiration platforms train people to perform taste rather than have it, collecting references "out of habit" instead of because "you simply can't look away." My reference gathering was performative in exactly this way: I named Mercury, Ramp and Linear as lineage and then copied surfaces from them.

Taste must be defensible. The strongest counter-argument I read (Miranda, "The obsession with taste is setting designers back") is that "you can't put taste in a ticket." Her fix is to argue from outcomes: not "this feels cleaner" but "this reduces the number of things Dara has to read before she knows what to do." I used words like authored, editorial and punch as if they were arguments. They aren't; they're moods.

Good design is redesign, and it can copy. Two of Graham's principles I under-used. Expect to throw the first version away. And copying confidently is fine, as long as you copy the logic and not the paint: Ramp's single weight is a rule about hierarchy, not a look; Stripe's rules-not-cards is a rule about scanning speed. A navy sidebar is paint.

What good looks like nowObserved, not remembered

I looked at the actual products, not their marketing: Ramp's live demo, Linear's app, Attio, Mercury's demo, Stripe's dashboard, Vercel. They differ in temperature (Ramp is large and airy; Linear and Attio are small and dense) but they share a discipline that is more important than the temperature.

TraitHow it shows up
One idea about hierarchyRamp: one weight, hierarchy from size alone. Linear: weights in a narrow 400–510 band, hierarchy from size and tone. Neither mixes bold, mono, caps and colour to signal importance. They pick one lever and pull it hard.
Chrome is nearly invisibleSidebars are tonal or white, never a brand block. Navigation is words with a faint selected state. The product is the content.
One accent, used as punctuationRamp's chartreuse on the primary action only. Stripe's indigo on links and the selected tile. Semantic colours (green/amber/red) exist separately and don't count as the accent.
Rules or tone, not both, and no shadowsSeparation comes from hairlines (Ramp, Stripe) or from surface tone (Mercury, Attio). Cards with border + radius + shadow on every block is the template signature they all avoid.
Density is a choice, applied everywhereRamp rows are 68px tall with two-line cells and vertical dividers; Attio rows are 32px with 13px type. Both are consistent to the pixel. Mixed density reads as unfinished.
Tables are the workhorseThe main surface of every B2B product I looked at is a table or a list, done extremely well. Stat-card rows and dashboards are secondary, when present at all.
Actions are words"View all · Create" as text links on a section header (Stripe). One solid button per screen. Icon buttons only in toolbars.
Numbers are typeset, not decoratedBig figures are the body face at 44–60px, tabular, tight tracking. No mono for its own sake, no rings or gauges unless the value is a proportion of a whole.
Motion carries meaningRauno's rule: skip animation on high-frequency interactions; use it where it explains where something came from. Nothing draws itself in for delight.

The rubricWhat I check before you see anything

Miranda's point taken: these are things that can be checked, not felt. A screen doesn't get shown until it passes.

01The brief exists and I can quote it. Who is using this, what is the one job of the screen, what should it feel like in three words, and which two references set the register. If I can't say it, I'm decorating.
02First-second test. At 50% zoom, the single most important thing on the screen is the first thing the eye lands on, and there is exactly one of them.
03One hierarchy lever. Importance is signalled by size, or by weight, or by tone. Not by three of them at once, and never by mono, caps or colour as extra emphasis.
04Count the colours. One accent. Semantic good/warn/bad. Chart hues only on charts. Anything else is a defect.
05Count the label systems. Section titles, column headers, field labels and meta text use one system, with at most two sizes between them.
06Separation is one mechanism. Hairlines or tone. If a block has a border, a radius and a shadow, two of them are unnecessary.
07Every element has a sentence. For each visual element: what does it tell Dara that nothing else on the screen tells her? Elements without a sentence come off. This is the test the ledger tabs, the eyebrows and the health rings all fail.
08Same-zoom comparison. Put the screen beside the reference at the same scale. List the differences in size, spacing and density as numbers. Close them or justify them.
09The system's own rules are obeyed. The labs' CLAUDE.md says selected states are blue-100 with navy text, hover is warm-gray-050, radii are 12/8/6. I broke all three for two weeks.
10Describe it without mood words. If my explanation of the screen needs "authored", "editorial", "punchy" or "premium" to make sense, the screen isn't done.

The pattern is the same each time: I treated feedback as a request for a different surface, when it was a request for a different judgment. And I showed work I hadn't critiqued myself, so you were doing my job.

ProposalHow I'd like to proceed

Step one · a brief, agreed before any pixels

One paragraph we both sign off on: Dara's job on this screen, the single thing she must see first, the register in three words, the two reference products, the density (Ramp-airy or Attio-dense), and the accent question answered. Fifteen minutes of conversation, not a document.

Step two · one screen, self-critiqued before you see it

The Partners screen only. I build it to the brief, run the rubric above, do the same-zoom comparison against the reference, and write down the three decisions I'm least sure of. You get the screen and those three questions, nothing else.

Step three · critique against the brief, not against taste

You tell me where it fails the brief. "Bad" is allowed, but I'll ask which line of the brief it fails, and I'll stop building and answer if I can't tell.

The one decision only you can make

Every reference with the energy you're asking for has a single vivid signal colour that the rest of the system is built to stay out of the way of. The Design Labs palette has navy, warm greys and a set of muted chart hues; it has no such colour. Either we add one (a system change, yours to make), or Partner Desk's "punch" has to come from scale and contrast alone, which is Stripe's answer rather than Ramp's. I'd rather ask than pick.
